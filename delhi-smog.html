Delhi's Winter Smog Crisis

By G.V. Kumar • Retd. Meteorologist, IMD, RMC Chennai • 5 February 2026

Introduction

Every winter, Delhi transforms into one of the world's most polluted cities. As temperatures drop and winds calm, a toxic blanket of smog envelops the metropolitan region, affecting over 30 million residents. The Air Quality Index (AQI) regularly exceeds 400—classified as 'severe' or 'hazardous'—with PM (particulate matter) 2.5 concentrations reaching 15-20 times the World Health Organization's safe limits. This annual crisis is not merely an environmental problem but a severe public health emergency that costs thousands of lives and affects the development of millions of children.

Understanding the chemistry behind smog formation, its dispersion mechanisms, and its devastating health impacts is crucial for developing effective solutions. This article explores the complex interplay of atmospheric chemistry, meteorology, and human activities that create Delhi's winter smog, and examines both the immediate and long-term consequences for public health.

The Science of Smog Formation

Understanding Winter Smog

Delhi's winter smog is a complex mixture of particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), carbon monoxide (CO), ozone (O₃), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Unlike the photochemical smog common in summer months, winter smog is dominated by primary pollutants that accumulate due to specific meteorological conditions and intensive heating activities.

The Role of Temperature Inversion

The critical factor in Delhi's winter air quality crisis is temperature inversion. Normally, air temperature decreases with altitude, allowing warm, polluted air to rise and disperse. However, during winter nights, the ground cools rapidly, creating a layer of cold, dense air near the surface trapped beneath warmer air above. This atmospheric lid prevents vertical mixing, trapping pollutants in the breathing zone for days or even weeks.

Chemical Processes in Smog Formation

Sulfur Dioxide Transformation

Sulfur dioxide, primarily from coal combustion, undergoes several transformation pathways:

Aqueous Phase Oxidation:
SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃
H₂SO₃ + ½O₂ → H₂SO₄

In daylight, hydroxyl radicals (OH•) facilitate gas-phase oxidation, but winter's weak sunlight severely limits this cleansing mechanism when Delhi needs it most.

Nitrogen Oxides Chemistry

Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) play a dual role. During daytime, NO₂ photolysis creates ozone (O₃). However, at night, fresh NO emissions from vehicles actually destroy ozone: O₃ + NO → NO₂ + O₂.

Nitric acid also converts to ammonium nitrate particles: HNO₃ + NH₃ → NH₄NO₃. These secondary particles significantly contribute to PM2.5 concentrations, especially in humid conditions.

Devastating Health Impacts

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) penetrates deep into the lungs and enters the bloodstream, triggering systemic inflammation and oxidative stress.

Respiratory and Cardiovascular Damage

Studies demonstrate that for every 10 μg/m³ increase in PM2.5 concentration, heart attack risk increases by 1-3%. During severe episodes, Delhi hospitals report surges in heart attacks and strokes.

Chronic exposure leads to irreversible lung damage. Children growing up in Delhi show permanently reduced lung capacity—a deficit that persists throughout life.

Neurological and Cognitive Impacts

Ultrafine particles can reach the brain, triggering neuroinflammation. This is linked to accelerated cognitive decline in adults and neurodevelopmental risks in children, affecting academic performance and behavior.

Emission Control Measures

Because natural cleansing fails in winter, controlling emissions at the source is imperative:

  • Transportation: Implementing BS-VI standards and promoting electric vehicles.
  • Industry: Installing electrostatic precipitators and flue gas desulfurization.
  • Agriculture: Providing machinery like Happy Seeders to prevent crop residue burning.
  • Urban Management: Dust control via anti-smog guns and water sprinkling.

Conclusion

Delhi's winter smog is a humanitarian crisis. The solution lies in aggressive, sustained emission reductions across all sectors—transport, industry, power, and agriculture—and strong regional coordination to ensure the fundamental right to breathe clean air.
