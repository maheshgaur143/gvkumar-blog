Delhi's Winter Smog Crisis

By G.V. Kumar • Retd. Meteorologist, IMD, RMC Chennai • 5 February 2026

Introduction

Every winter, Delhi transforms into one of the world's most polluted cities. As temperatures drop and winds calm, a toxic blanket of smog envelops the metropolitan region, affecting over 30 million residents. The Air Quality Index (AQI) regularly exceeds 400—classified as 'severe' or 'hazardous'—with PM (particulate matter) 2.5 concentrations reaching 15-20 times the World Health Organization's safe limits.

This annual crisis is not merely an environmental problem but a severe public health emergency that costs thousands of lives and affects the development of millions of children. Understanding the chemistry behind smog formation, its dispersion mechanisms, and its devastating health impacts is crucial for developing effective solutions.

The Science of Smog Formation

Understanding Winter Smog

Delhi's winter smog is a complex mixture of particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), carbon monoxide (CO), ozone (O₃), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Unlike photochemical smog, winter smog is dominated by primary pollutants that accumulate due to specific meteorological conditions and intensive heating activities.

The Role of Temperature Inversion

The critical factor is temperature inversion. Normally, air temperature decreases with altitude. However, during winter nights, the ground cools rapidly, creating a layer of cold, dense air near the surface trapped beneath warmer air above. This atmospheric lid prevents vertical mixing, trapping pollutants in the breathing zone for days or even weeks.

Chemical Processes in Smog Formation

Sulfur Dioxide Transformation

Sulfur dioxide (SO₂), primarily from coal combustion, undergoes transformation into sulfate particles:

Aqueous Phase Oxidation:
SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃
H₂SO₃ + ½O₂ → H₂SO₄

In daylight, hydroxyl radicals (OH•) facilitate gas-phase oxidation, but winter's weak sunlight severely limits this cleansing mechanism when Delhi needs it most.

Nitrogen Oxides Chemistry

Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) play a dual role. During daytime, NO₂ photolysis creates ozone (O₃). However, at night, fresh NO emissions actually destroy ozone: O₃ + NO → NO₂ + O₂.

Nitric acid also converts to ammonium nitrate particles: HNO₃ + NH₃ → NH₄NO₃. These secondary particles significantly contribute to PM2.5 concentrations, especially in humid conditions.

Carbon Monoxide and Particulates

Carbon monoxide (CO) from incomplete combustion poses immediate health threats by binding to haemoglobin more effectively than oxygen, reducing blood oxygen-carrying capacity. In winter fog, these particles absorb water through hygroscopic growth, creating the characteristic thick haze.

Devastating Health Impacts

The Scale of the Crisis: Studies estimate 10,000-30,000 premature deaths annually in Delhi. Long-term residents may see life expectancy reduced by 10-12 years. During severe episodes, daily mortality increases by 2-5%.

Respiratory and Cardiovascular Damage

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) penetrates deep into the lungs and enters the bloodstream, triggering systemic inflammation. For every 10 μg/m³ increase in PM2.5, heart attack risk increases by 1-3%.

Chronic exposure leads to irreversible lung damage. Children in Delhi show permanently reduced lung capacity compared to those in less polluted areas.

Neurological and Cognitive Impacts

Ultrafine particles can cross the blood-brain barrier, triggering neuroinflammation. This is linked to accelerated cognitive decline in adults (including risks of dementia and Alzheimer's) and neurodevelopmental risks in children, affecting academic performance and behavior.

Vulnerable Populations

  • Children: Breathe more rapidly and have developing organs. About 43.5% of school children in Delhi suffer from restrictive or poor lung function.
  • Elderly: Highest mortality risk due to reduced physiological reserves and pre-existing conditions.
  • Pregnant Women: Associated with increased rates of low-birth-weight babies and preterm births.

Systemic and Economic Burden

Chronic exposure is linked to increased diabetes risk, reduced kidney function, and decreased fertility. Economically, this manifests as surging healthcare expenditures, productivity losses from illness, and widespread school closures.

Emission Control Measures at Source

Effective management requires a multi-sectoral approach targeting all major pollution sources:

Transportation Sector

Implementation of Bharat Stage VI (BS-VI) norms using three-way catalytic converters, diesel particulate filters (DPF), and selective catalytic reduction (SCR) systems. Promotion of electric vehicles and expansion of the metro network are critical.

Industrial and Power Generation

Industries utilize electrostatic precipitators, bag filters, and wet scrubbers. For power plants, mandatory flue gas desulfurization (FGD) and transition to supercritical technology or renewable energy (solar, wind) are essential.

Agriculture and Waste

Promoting "Happy Seeders" and balers to manage crop residue without burning. Urban waste management focuses on proper segregation and processing facilities to prevent open burning of plastics.

Construction and Dust Management

Mandatory water spraying, anti-smog guns, and mechanical sweeping of roads. Indoor air quality is improved via HEPA filtration and activated carbon.

Regulatory Framework and Hydrology

The Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) implements escalating restrictions. On 13th December 2025, Stage 4 (AQI > 450) was enforced, banning construction and older diesel vehicles.

A critical overlooked factor is groundwater depletion. With nearly 99% of groundwater extracted, the decline in the water table leads to drier soil in winter, which significantly increases airborne dust (PM10 and PM2.5) emissions.

Conclusion

Delhi's winter smog is a humanitarian crisis. The solution lies not in waiting for nature, but in aggressive, sustained emission reductions across all sectors—transport, industry, power, and agriculture—and strong regional coordination to ensure the fundamental right to breathe clean air.
