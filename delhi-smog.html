Delhi's Winter Smog Crisis

By G.V. Kumar • Retd. Meteorologist, IMD, RMC Chennai • 5 February 2026

Introduction

Every winter, Delhi transforms into one of the world's most polluted cities. As temperatures drop and winds calm, a toxic blanket of smog envelops the metropolitan region, affecting over 30 million residents. The Air Quality Index (AQI) regularly exceeds 400—classified as 'severe' on India's AQI scale—with PM (particulate matter) 2.5 concentrations during the worst episodes reaching 15-20 times the World Health Organization's 24-hour guideline value of 15 µg/m³.

This annual crisis is not merely an environmental problem but a severe public health emergency that costs thousands of lives and affects the development of millions of children. Understanding the chemistry behind smog formation, its dispersion mechanisms, and its devastating health impacts is crucial for developing effective solutions.

The Science of Smog Formation

Understanding Winter Smog

Delhi's winter smog is a complex mixture of particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), carbon monoxide (CO), ozone (O₃), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Unlike photochemical smog, winter smog is dominated by primary pollutants and by secondary particles formed in cold, humid air, all of which accumulate due to specific meteorological conditions and intensive combustion and heating activities.

The Role of Temperature Inversion

The critical factor is temperature inversion. Normally, air temperature decreases with altitude, allowing warm, polluted air to rise and disperse. However, during winter nights, the ground cools rapidly, creating a layer of cold, dense air near the surface trapped beneath warmer air above. This atmospheric lid prevents vertical mixing, trapping pollutants in the breathing zone for days or even weeks. Combined with low wind speeds typical of North Indian winters, pollutants accumulate to dangerous concentrations.

Chemical Processes in Smog Formation

Sulfur Dioxide Transformation

Sulfur dioxide (SO₂), primarily from coal combustion in power plants and industries, undergoes transformation into sulfate particles. While ambient SO₂ concentrations in Delhi are generally modest compared with NOₓ and particulate matter, the resulting sulfate forms a notable part of secondary PM2.5.

Aqueous Phase Oxidation (in fog droplets):
SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃
H₂SO₃ + ½O₂ → H₂SO₄

This process is accelerated by metal catalysts (iron, manganese) present on particulate matter. In daylight, hydroxyl radicals (OH•) facilitate gas-phase oxidation, but winter's weak sunlight and short days severely limit this cleansing mechanism when Delhi needs it most.

Nitrogen Oxides Chemistry

Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) play a dual role. During daytime, NO₂ photolysis creates ozone (O₃). However, at night and near busy roads, fresh NO emissions from vehicles actually destroy ozone: O₃ + NO → NO₂ + O₂.

Nitric acid also converts to ammonium nitrate particles: HNO₃ + NH₃ ↔ NH₄NO₃. This reaction is reversible and favored by cold, humid air, making ammonium nitrate prominent in winter and contributing significantly to PM2.5 concentrations.

Carbon Monoxide and Particulates

Carbon monoxide (CO) from incomplete combustion poses immediate health threats by binding to haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen, reducing blood oxygen-carrying capacity. In winter fog, particles absorb water through hygroscopic growth, creating a thick haze that reduces visibility to mere meters and concentrates toxic substances.

Devastating Health Impacts

Scale of the Health Crisis: One study estimated 7,350-16,200 premature deaths annually in Delhi (for 2010). The Air Quality Life Index suggests that if current levels persist, the average resident could lose about 8.2 years of life expectancy relative to WHO guidelines.

Respiratory System Damage

PM2.5 (fine particles ≤ 2.5µm) can cause systemic respiratory and cardiovascular issues, while PM10 (inhalable particles ≤ 10µm) are deposited in the nose and throat. Emergency room visits for asthma attacks increase dramatically, with airways becoming inflamed and producing excess mucus.

Chronic exposure leads to irreversible lung damage. Studies of Delhi schoolchildren show reduced lung function (affecting about 43.5% of children vs 25.7% of rural controls), and deficits acquired in childhood can persist into adulthood.

Cardiovascular System Damage

Ultrafine particles (smaller than 0.1 micrometre) penetrate deep into the lungs and may cross into the bloodstream, triggering systemic inflammation. For every 10 μg/m³ short-term increase in PM2.5, heart attack risk increases by roughly 1-3%. Long-term exposure accelerates atherosclerosis and cardiovascular aging.

Neurological and Cognitive Impacts

Ultrafine particles can reach the brain via the blood-brain barrier or olfactory nerves, triggering neuroinflammation. This is linked to accelerated cognitive decline, increased risks of dementia and Alzheimer's, and reduced brain volume in memory-related areas.

Children face severe neurodevelopmental risks, including reduced academic performance and possible links to autism spectrum disorders, with measurable differences in white matter development.

Vulnerable Populations

  • Children: Breathe more rapidly relative to body weight and have more vulnerable developing organs.
  • Elderly: Highest mortality risk due to reduced physiological reserves and pre-existing conditions.
  • Pregnant Women: Associated with increased rates of low-birth-weight babies, preterm births, and fetal brain development impacts.

Systemic and Economic Burden

Exposure is linked to increased diabetes risk, reduced kidney function, and decreased fertility. Economically, Delhi had the highest per-capita economic loss from air pollution in 2019, estimated at US$46.6-79.9 per person.

Emission Control Measures at Source

Since natural cleansing fails in winter, controlling emissions is imperative. A comprehensive approach includes:

Transportation Sector

Implementation of Bharat Stage VI (BS-VI) norms (comparable to Euro 6), mandating three-way catalytic converters, diesel particulate filters (trapping >95% of particulates), and selective catalytic reduction (reducing NOₓ by up to 90%). Success depends on ultra-low sulfur fuels and preventing tampering.

Industrial and Power Generation

Industries use electrostatic precipitators (capturing 90-99% of particles), bag filters, and wet scrubbers. Power plants require flue gas desulfurization (FGD) for SO₂ control: SO₂ + CaCO₃ + ½O₂ → CaSO₄ + CO₂. Transitioning to supercritical technology and renewables (solar, wind) is the long-term solution.

Agriculture and Waste

Providing farmers with Happy Seeders, balers, and rakes to manage crop residue without burning. Urban waste management focuses on proper segregation and composting to prevent the open burning of plastics.

Construction and Dust Management

Mandatory water spraying, anti-smog guns, wheel washing, and mechanized sweeping. Indoor protection includes HEPA filtration with activated carbon. Note that negative ion technology cannot be scaled to clear city-level smog.

Regulatory Framework and Hydrology

The Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) implements escalating restrictions. On 13th December 2025, Stage IV (AQI > 450) was invoked, banning construction and older diesel vehicles.

An under-recognized factor is groundwater depletion. With nearly 99% of groundwater extracted (as of 2023), the declining water table weakens capillary rise and soil moisture. This dry soil contributes significantly to airborne dust (PM10 and PM2.5). Impervious surface expansion further reduces the capacity of soil and vegetation to suppress dust.

Conclusion

Delhi's winter smog is a humanitarian crisis. The solution lies not in waiting for nature, but in aggressive, sustained emission reductions across all sectors—transport, industry, power, and agriculture—and strong regional coordination to ensure the fundamental right to breathe clean air.
